Visualization Critique and Redesign

Where America Lives: redesigning a population-density map

This page critiques TIME's 3D population-density map and redesigns it as an interactive D3 choropleth that makes county-level comparison and low-density variation easier to inspect.

Original visualization

Title and source: Where America Lives, TIME graphic by Feilding Cage, TIME USA, LLC. (2019 copyright footer; publication date unverified).

Data shown: U.S. population density across local areas (the original spatial unit and data year are unverified), with tall red spikes marking dense metropolitan regions and flatter areas representing sparse population.

Intended message: The original annotation states that 80% of Americans live in metropolitan areas; this is a historical claim from the graphic, not a result calculated here. The map emphasizes the contrast between dense coastal / urban corridors and the sparse interior West and Midwest.

Critique summary

Strengths: The original map has strong visual hierarchy and an immediately legible message: urban America appears as isolated peaks against a mostly low-density landmass. Color and height work together to attract attention to dense metropolitan areas.

Weaknesses: The 3D spikes make precise comparison difficult, occlude nearby regions, and compress low-density counties into a nearly indistinguishable baseline. Labels and legend are also not sufficient for county-level lookup.

Redesign logic

The redesign uses a 2D county choropleth, persistent county details, keyboard-accessible lookup, a linked density histogram, and a scale toggle. It preserves the national concentration story while making comparison, lookup, and low-density exploration more explicit.

Data and references

Density = July 1, 2020 population estimate ÷ official 2020 Gazetteer land area in square miles. These are population estimates, not decennial census counts. Data and scripts are bundled locally. The map uses simplified 2017 us-atlas boundaries. Chugach and Copper River are combined to match the older Valdez-Cordova area. The 3,142 mapped areas cover the 50 states and DC; territories are excluded.

County averages hide variation within counties, and large rural polygons dominate the map's area. Colors support grouped comparison; use numeric details for values. This is an equivalent-data redesign, not a reconstruction of TIME's historical measurements.
