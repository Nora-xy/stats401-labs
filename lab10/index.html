READING THE TWO VIEWS

Geographic extent ≠ economic weight

The United States and China stand out in both views: they have the darkest fills and the largest GDP circles. On the choropleth, Russia, Canada, and Australia also attract attention because their geographic footprints are large, even though their GDP is far below that of the United States.

In the Dorling cartogram, Germany, Japan, the United Kingdom, and especially compact economies such as Singapore become more prominent relative to their land area. Russia, Canada, and Australia lose geographic dominance. Circle area is directly proportional to GDP, while radius is proportional to its square root. This makes economic magnitude more explicit than color alone.

The choropleth preserves country outlines, adjacency, and recognizable geographic placement, subject to projection distortion. It supports questions about where high-output economies are located and how GDP varies across regions. A logarithmic color scale separates the many smaller values instead of letting the United States and China compress most countries into similar pale shades; equal color steps represent multiplicative differences.

The cartogram better supports comparisons of total economic size. It replaces boundaries with circles and displaces their centers to prevent overlap, sacrificing exact distances, shapes, and some neighborhood relationships. Linked highlighting preserves identity across these changes. Together, the views show why a country's apparent size on a conventional map can be a poor guide to its share of economic output.

DESIGN REFLECTION

A voice shortcut to small economies

I extended Lab 9's linked GDP choropleth and Dorling cartogram, retaining its data, geographic projection, proportional circles, legends, and conventional interactions. The new modality is browser speech recognition. Commands such as “Highlight Japan” select the same economy in both views and center the geographic map; “Zoom in,” “Zoom out,” and “Reset” support navigation.

Voice supports locating a named economy without searching through small boundaries or opening a long dropdown. This is particularly useful for Singapore and Hong Kong, whose geographic footprints are difficult to click. Spoken and typed commands invoke the existing visualization controls, so selection and zoom behave consistently across input methods.

Automated checks exposed the need to reject unknown country names without changing the current selection and to recover controls after permission or recognition errors. Actual microphone recognition has not been tested here, so accent and background-noise performance remain unmeasured. Browser support and remote speech services can also limit availability. Visible transcript, action, and error feedback make the interpretation inspectable, while typed commands, buttons, and keyboard selection preserve access when speech fails.
